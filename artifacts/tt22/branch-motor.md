# Learning later-rally strokes from physical practice branches

On 10 fresh native points, the later-stroke selector learned from physically branched practice returned the ball legally a second time on 6 points. The matched selector using stage and per-stroke marginals did so on 1 point. Five paired points improved and none regressed on that measure. Both conditions made all 10 first returns, with identical optical observations and commands for both arms through the first within-rally decision. The longest rally was 6 alternating legal returns. The learner uses two calibrated native RGB cameras, optical flight estimates, a supplied SO-101 factory kinematic model and the public commands it has sent. It chooses one depth, face loft, blade pace and fractional lead for each incoming leg. An inverse-kinematics rule converts the selected stroke to six joint-rate commands, including zero extra gripper motion. The learner rearms when the cameras see the ball moving outbound past the net. There are no simulated ball–blade collision queries, contact flags or target simulator states in its live input. The cooperative rival remains the previously prepared five-axis physical controller, with target profile `[0.0, -0.90, 0.20, 2.40]` and the earlier wider release family (spread .55, pace .90).

Training reconstructed 18 earlier native donor points by replaying both arms' commands to the visual cue. At each eligible cue, a training-only evaluator saved the complete MuJoCo integration state and referee state. It restored that same state for each kinematically reachable action in a fixed grid of 144 alternatives and ran actual colliders and the strict referee for up to 39 control frames. The far arm followed the archived rates over the common available suffix and held its target thereafter. The outcome was whether the trainee's next bounce was strictly legal and where it landed. Each alternative stopped at the first legal return, fault, or horizon. The far-arm suffix and full-state restoration apply only to practice. Fresh trials used neither and played from ordinary initial serves to terminal point outcomes without mid-point resets.

The 18 donor tapes reproduced the original scores and full referee events. Each of the 45 later branch cues followed a physical opponent hit. The original donor recordings span 2,473 control frames. A legal branch exists at 41 of the 45 later cues. The kernel scorer uses optical position, velocity, predicted crossing and blade pose from integrated commands. It learns per-stroke utility from legal outcomes with a smaller credit for central placement after a legal score. Holding out one entire donor from the corpus at a time, it chooses physically legal branches at 23 of 45 later cues versus 7 of 45 for the same scorer with optical context removed (16 of 18 opening cues for the shared stage-only approach). This diagnostic omits the small shared IK alignment penalty and selects among feasible branches at the held donor cue. It measures interpolation across archived donors.

The main fresh comparison shares the exact same opening policy, candidate generation and stagewise outcome marginals. Only the contextual correction on later strokes differs. Both use the signed lateral coefficients from the earlier native-duration Pong checkpoint, approximately `(1.014, −.547)`, and pass those through the same conservative 24% correction to geometric lateral aim. The relative origin is the factory neutral blade x coordinate. The Pong term scales the current observed ball offset from that origin and the projected lateral movement over the time to the depth plane. The actor-relative travel limit is ±.105 m. An additional ±.065 m cap limits disagreements with geometric aim before the 24% blend. The earlier collision-planned donor controllers, supplied calibration and motion basis, thousands of branched attempts, and the scripted evaluator rival all contribute to this result.
