# Receiving on the table-edge SO-101

## What the learner experienced

The near arm sees two simulated external RGB cameras in the initial long-rally test. It reads its six joint encoders and remembers its issued commands. A calibrated image tracker estimates the moving ball from pixels. A separate nominal simulation proposes movements and checks whether they would reach the opposite half. Whenever the real game awards a legal return, the learner saves its commanded joint positions, estimated incoming flight and time to contact. On a subsequent approach it times candidate actions from memory against the new observations. Gravity, the geometry of the robot and racket, camera registration, the orange-ball detector, the receiving objective and an initial motion planner are supplied. The learner uses no backpropagation. The far opponent sees the true ball to control a finite-mass physical paddle. The ball is launched once per point.

Four measured openings chosen for receiving practice were run consecutively. The resulting points contained **0, 0, 14 and 66** alternating legal returns. On the last opening, the arm entered with seven remembered motions and finished with 40. It used four fallback searches on the 14-return point and six on the 66-return point, in addition to selecting motions from memory. These counts alone cannot establish the benefit of memory. [Command tapes, per-point results and checkpoint](camera-action-memory.json.gz)

The 66 returns comprised 33 near and 33 far landings in one point. The median departure speed was 5.63 m/s, with 5.29 m/s from the SO-101. Median time between hits was 0.675 s. On its next stroke the near arm sent the ball into its own half. Deterministic replay reproduced 337 events and yielded positive clearance at all 21,952 audited states. The separate DeepMind dataset has a median outgoing speed of 5.50 m/s across 13,088 recorded shots. This comparison establishes a physical pace reference for the simulation. The 66-return game lasted 45.7 simulated seconds and took 183 seconds of wall time in the shared execution environment. ## Why the arm and the opponent matter

We drew 384 incoming recorded shots without centering them. Of 367 that bounced on the near half, 29 stayed outside even a generous rigid-arm reach sphere on the new mount. The archived mount left 122 outside the same bound. Being inside the sphere only establishes a possible opportunity. Orienting the blade, avoiding the arm itself and reaching the ball before it passes impose further restrictions. The cooperative partner varies its returns within a forehand region that a clamped SO-101 can reach. [Paired reach calculation and individual shot IDs](workspace.json.gz) · [Partner reach and pace](partner.md)

A centered, deeply folded posture produced 41 alternating legal landings in the referee. The CAD audit found 33 mm of wrist–shoulder overlap. We excluded that point from the performance evidence. Moving the receiving position laterally allowed a forehand without that overlap. A separate side-on grip fits the physical handle between both jaws and provides another way to reach low balls. [Fixture and grip photographs from MuJoCo](fixture.md) · [Folded-posture diagnostic](rally-physics-folded-diagnostic.json.gz)

We tested whether selecting one ready posture or one repeated motor action would suffice. On one nominal practice feed, 378 stationary candidate blocks produced zero legal landings. Adding a timed joint drive produced 156 successful candidates out of 768. Of those, 40 passed an initial posture/path clearance screen. The selected clear motion returned 9/24 independently drawn openings on the stock plant, 1/24 after changing materials, and 5/24 after changing the arm load. Physical state was available when proposing these candidates. These trials motivate continuous camera feedback during a rally.
