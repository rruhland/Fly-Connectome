# Can the arm reuse what worked in earlier rallies?

On twelve distinct measured openings, the arm made 14 legal returns with memory frozen empty, 26 while storing physically scored actions from these games, and 32 with additional memory of 86 earlier successes. Each incoming target was drawn independently. The simulated opponent had to intercept the previous return to create the next one. The longest point in the cold learning run contained nine arm returns and ten opposing returns. [Opponent and court](opponent.md) · [Twelve assigned openings](school-plan.json)

The controller first tests nearby motor corrections that previously earned a legal landing. It simulates them in a separate nominal robot initialized from camera observations, the arm's encoder angles and the setpoints it has actually issued. If none of those alternatives is predicted to land, it searches additional joint corrections. Only actual table landings update motor memory. Its geometric stroke generator and nominal physics are supplied priors. All three arms started from the same joint angles and used the same simulation budgets and randomization rule. Across whole games their simulated time differed because better play kept the ball alive. Total CPU time was 59.8, 82.4 and 89.4 seconds for 45.3, 66.3 and 73.3 seconds of simulated play, respectively. These timings include stereo rendering, opponent and arena physics. ## Learning a correction from subsequent camera frames

The learner can update its visual estimate on scoreless frames. Every target uses a later observation of the ball, and the update becomes available three frames after the source observation. Learning how the ball rebounds from 140 observed practice bounces reduced vertical velocity error to 0.041 m/s on 283 held receiving frames, from 0.366 m/s originally. An additional free-flight smoother increased that error to 0.051 m/s. The original calibrated rebound estimator remained the default for the memory comparison. [Learned rebound](rebound-quality.json) · [Smoother](flight-fit-quality.json)

At 33 early physical decision states we also replayed 39 candidate joint commands per state and compared predictions against native outcomes. For the exact candidates proposed from the improved sensor, the optical body model recognized 170 of 384 legal candidates, with 229 predicted legal. Replacing the sensed translation and velocity with the true values recognized 325, and also providing spin recognized all 384. At seven later decision states, the optical body model recognized 61 of 75 legal alternatives and predicted 103 legal. These are grouped diagnostics on practice states. They explain why the learner can choose a stroke it believes will land and still miss. [Matched native predictions and substitutions](replay-gated.json.gz)

## What transferred into camera-to-joint feedback

On 24 held releases, the 289-return direct pupil landed 16 total arm returns. Adding demonstrations from the same 14 practice starts in two ways produced different effects. Extra coach-controlled practice produced 19 returns on the 24 new openings and 20 on a separate set of 16 familiar openings with new replies. Letting the pupil approach the ball itself before the coach intervened produced 13 and 28 on those same courses. A shift in the practice poses benefited the familiar openings and cost performance on new ones. The design follows the observation that a learner visits different states under its own actions. [Training on learner-visited states](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v15/ross11a.html) · [Matched coach demonstrations](matched-teacher-rgb.json) · [Matched intervention demonstrations](matched-rollin-rgb.json)

A second direct learner predicted overlapping sequences of joint targets and averaged estimates for the current tick. On the 24 novel releases it produced 12 returns versus 16 for single-tick feedback, while on the 16 familiar openings it produced 20 versus 15. Calibrating joint commands against the effect they actually have on the paddle remains a higher priority.
