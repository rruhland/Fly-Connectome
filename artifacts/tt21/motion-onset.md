# Estimating lag from ordinary motion without a second renderer

Each update compares the first substantial requested joint movement with the onset of clearly visible motion on the same point. The stationary outside camera measures changes in the red paddle silhouette. The moving wrist camera measures changes to background patches. The detector estimates background activity before the stroke and looks for two successive movement samples above it. With no quiet interval or no clear onset, it keeps the previous delay. It updates after the point. The first changed point is therefore played with the previous estimate. [Estimator](../../scripts/experimental/tt21_motion_memory.py)

This routine needs no dry motor replay, extra target frames, or target score for the delay estimate. The arm still uses its inherited CAD model, optical geometry and physics planner to *play*. A competing learner used a recent command window to predict visible motion from similar past examples. On the four archived games that approach selected the correct lag at the end of 21 of 58 points. The simpler onset detector did so on 54/58, including all 31 wrist points. Those comparisons used previously collected RGB motion and commands. [Historical audit](motion-memory-history.json.gz)

We then ran complete live games on a fresh launch sequence, changing the hidden actuator delay from one frame at the opening to five after four points and to two after nine. The learner received no change signal. In both single-camera games it selected the correct lag after the first point of every installation and held it through that block. Both branches used the same game seed and strict service changes. Each full tape reproduced the commands delivered through the FIFO and the scorer events.

| Eye | Frozen → online game score | Legal first returns | Legal own serves | Total time spent estimating lag across the adaptive game |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Wrist | 0–11 → 3–11 | 0 → 1 | 2/5 → 4/6 | 5.7 ms |
| Outside | 1–11 → 0–11 | 0 → 1 | 1/6 → 3/5 | 2.5 ms |

Game lengths differed after the scores diverged. The wrist time and outside time cover only the per-point delay estimation from recorded motion energies. Rendering the live camera, measuring changes in its image, and computing strokes still take separate time. On the outside camera, improved legality again failed to translate to more points. These are two simulated games on one newly chosen delay schedule with a known arm and eyes. Lighting changes, image latency and real hardware backlash remain untested. In fresh closed-loop reruns of the original two wrist games, the onset estimator reproduced all 31 points (1,834 frames), including every RGB digest, requested and applied motor command, observation, referee event and outcome. The scores remained 5–11 and 4–11. Across the two games the earlier estimator rendered 1,143 extra nominal frames and spent 35.31 seconds estimating delay. The onset estimator used zero extra renders and spent 0.0082 seconds on delay estimation. Both used the same live image measurement and the same stroke planner. The timings cover the estimator in these runs; host contention can affect wall time.
