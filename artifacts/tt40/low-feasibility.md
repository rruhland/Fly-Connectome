# Can the mounted SO-101 rescue the lowest development balls?

This is a privileged **physical feasibility** test, not a visual controller or held-policy benchmark. The intact broad-random development court delivered 15 balls below the existing projected-height threshold of 0.99 m. The original arm touched 14 and returned 2 legally. The test chooses five failed later deliveries from that band. It takes the three lowest and the two remaining lateral extremes. Earlier completed exchanges, the incoming opponent aim and the complete issued command prefix are unchanged. No held opponent seed or evaluation release is used.

Two searches test different ways to reach a low ball. The early search uses exact physical flight after the far-racket separation, allowing eight contact depths from −1.11 to −1.70 m, both signed blade faces, and variants in pitch, drive and timing. The second search starts at the known table separation or an earlier nearby prefix. It varies the actual issued near-contact motion, so it can preserve an existing reachable approach instead of having to rediscover the body posture. The physical search outputs bounded direct joint-rate sequences. It does not move the clamp, collision geometry, table, ball or opponent. The isolated native search excludes the far blade only in candidate screening. In the final physical fork, the live far blade is present and reacts normally.

A rescue counts only if the changed commands produce a new audited near-side legal landing **and** the unchanged opponent physically answers with a legal near-side bounce. The actual game, not the private trajectory score, supplies both conditions. The replay then records only observable stereo detections, encoder readings and issued commands in the separate example archive.

The broad early search evaluated 6,912 native trajectories, including 3,365 positive-face and 3,547 negative-face trials. It found 24 legal candidates, on 8519. The archived-touch search evaluated 6,336 native trajectories and found 17 legal candidates, on 13052 and 8519. Only the three issued continuations count as physical evidence. All three actually scored and got a physical answer. The other three original misses did not get a legal candidate from these searches, so no rescue was issued on those legs. These finite results do not establish that the low balls are mechanically impossible.

On 8519 the early solution raised contact height by about 5 cm by taking the ball 26.5 cm sooner. The later alternative corrected the same failure from closer to the original contact. On 13052 the effective replacement was 3 cm deeper, so moving forward is not a universal remedy. The source controller already touched four of these five balls. Poor departure after a touch, not only interception, is the limiting failure here.

The verified answer on each new branch is an additional physical far racket collision and legal near-side landing, using the original opponent's next random draw. The saved near action tape replays from the original launch without resetting inside the point.

As a final falsification attempt, a local signed inverse of the simulated outgoing velocity guided a further 1,920 trajectories on 8302 and 14843. It generated 1,124 contacts but no legal landing. The broad, archived-touch and guided searches together evaluated 15,168 isolated native trajectories on the five original misses. The verified structural conclusion remains **two rescues out of five chosen misses**. The three successful physical tapes are alternate development examples, not evidence of generalization to unseen seeds.

All three rescue tapes reproduced their complete solver event histories exactly in separate replays. The incoming far collision, near bounce time and near bounce location matched the original offer exactly. The three observable replay groups contain 345 control ticks and 172 stereo exposures. They store only encoder readings, issued joint commands and camera-derived observations, with a separate legal-and-answered outcome flag.
