# Direct control and transfer

All physical scores are produced by the simulator. Each condition acts in its own point or drop, with paired starts. Evaluate five continuously commanded arm axes. The sixth servo keeps hold of the paddle.

## Arm adaptation

Use previously unseen continuous motor mixing matrices, unequal gains, a changed lower-arm mass, wrist camera load and one control tick of latency. Apply two changes and a return to the original body without notifying the sensorimotor learner. Compare the learner to its frozen nominal inverse on exactly the same stream of encoder goals. Report angular error at the end of each goal, drive time including probing, recovery during both changes, and performance after restoring the original body. This measures motor control with accessible joint observations. The game test separately measures the rebound count of the learned visual policy with and without an empirical command decoder. Three or four drops spent on calibration count as target experience. Independent oracle decoding is a diagnostic upper comparator and cannot be counted as learned recovery.

Four exploratory reaching seeds 431–434 were used to choose the probe duration, balanced directions and regularization. Confirm the reaching protocol on seeds 7101–7124. In the physical bounce comparison, use freshly sampled releases, identical changed-body schedules across treatments and a corrected wrist detector. Compare live control to stationary and blind as well as the Pong and shuffled learners. Record independent practice, demonstrations, missed drops and simulated time wherever applicable.

## Game evidence

For the rally pupil, an identical supplied legal opening isolates the next return. Report the second, fifth and tenth near-arm returns and full rallies. A pupil trained with action demonstrations is evidence for execution from live sensors. Reward-only Pong and bounce experiments address a different question about learning an objective and transfer. A source mechanism must beat matched flat and shuffled alternatives on the physical task to count as positive transfer. A longer isolated point cannot establish rapid adaptation across bodies.

An initial 24-stream reach test found that a badly predicted movement could press the paddle into the table or clamp before recalibration. On twelve reused development streams, reducing the size of uncertain commands and reversing an unexpectedly ineffective movement helped. The next reaching test uses seeds 83101–83132, a 0.40 command-vector norm bound, recall attempts during calibration and reversal after a surprising displacement. Both original and frozen controllers receive the same command-vector bound in that comparison.

The cross-eye test fits the same direct demonstration pupil from a single eye, starting at the first frame. A separate pupil learns a correspondence from one clip of simultaneous wrist and outside images and uses the other eye's demonstrated motor experience. Neither both cameras nor action labels from that paired clip are required when executing the transferred policy. A direct one-clip pupil, a full demonstration pupil and delayed visual observations provide comparisons. Every scorer replays the actual joint commands and referee.

Work on actual human racket bouncing found that timing can make recurring bounces dynamically stable. This motivated testing a generic recurrent motor memory driven by sensor changes and previous movements. A physical impact phase or a racket-acceleration rule would supply part of the solution and is excluded from this learner.
